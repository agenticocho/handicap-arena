# FINDING 003 — Best of 325

**Source analysed:** Miles Deutscher, *"I tested 50 AI Trading Bots. One Completely Dominated
(insane gains)"*, X Article, 6 Oct 2026, 53.8K views. Companion to `GAMES.md` (League J) and to
`FINDING_001/002`.
**Script:** `best_of_325.py` · **Status:** quantified, reproducible

---

## What the article does

Aimed at people building AI trading bots, it addresses the fair objection *"just because you build
a bot doesn't mean you'll be profitable."* The method:

1. **Source 50 strategies free** — TradingView community Pine Script, Stonehill Forex (NNFX
   frameworks), QuantConnect, Quantpedia (academic papers → strategies).
2. **Inject into Claude** — convert Pine Script to backtestable code; for the rest, generate one
   markdown spec per strategy (asset, timeframe, entry, exit, stop, sizing, **flag assumptions**).
3. **Build a backtesting engine** — run every spec across assets and timeframes, **including fees**,
   then rank on total return, max drawdown, win rate, profit factor, trade count, and **performance
   vs buy-and-hold**.
4. **325+ backtests** across the 50 strategies.
5. **Re-verify the shortlist in TradingView** — explicitly *"to confirm Claude didn't hallucinate
   during the backtest."*
6. **Forward-test** — paper, then small live, then scale.
7. **Winner:** a Stonehill strategy — beat buy-and-hold, **55% of trades profitable, max drawdown
   −8.86%, profit factor 1.9.**

## The finding

**The winner was selected as the maximum of ~325 attempts, and the article never asks what the
maximum of 325 attempts looks like under a null of no edge at all.**

Simulated: a zero-edge strategy (p = 0.5, +1R/−1R, expected value exactly zero), searched 325 times,
repeated 4,000 times per trade count:

| trades (n) | P(single zero-edge PF ≥ 1.9) | median best-of-325 | 95th pct best-of-325 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 0.0574 | **4.00** | 9.00 |
| 30 | 0.0495 | 3.29 | 5.00 |
| 50 | 0.0166 | 2.33 | 3.17 |
| 75 | 0.0027 | **2.00** | 2.41 |
| 100 | 0.0009 | 1.78 | 2.12 |
| 200 | ~0 | 1.50 | 1.67 |
| 500 | ~0 | 1.29 | 1.38 |

**If the winning strategy has fewer than ~75 trades, a profit factor of 1.9 is *below* what pure
noise typically produces when you take the best of 325.** It is not weak evidence of an edge — it is
worse than the expected order statistic of no edge at all.

**And the article does not report n for the winner.** It lists "number of trades" as one of the
engine's output metrics and then omits it from the result it headlines. **The single number that
would settle whether the winner is real is the one number withheld** — the same defect as an
unspecified baseline, in a different costume.

One number does move in the article's favour: 55% win rate against a 1.9 profit factor implies a
modest reward:risk, and drawdown of −8.86% is not the signature of a curve-fit blowup. So the
strategy may well be real. **The point is that nothing in the article can distinguish "real" from
"best of 325," and the missing n is what makes it undecidable.**

## What the article gets right

- **It names the fabrication failure and routes around it.** *"Verify them on TradingView… just to
  confirm Claude didn't hallucinate during the backtest."* An LLM's self-reported backtest is a
  claim about an LLM, and the author treats it as one. This is the single most useful line in the piece.
- **Buy-and-hold is the stated baseline.** The null is not "did it make money" but "did it beat not
  playing." That is the correct question and most bot content never asks it.
- **Fees are in the engine.** Most retail strategy content is gross-only.
- **Structured specs with flagged assumptions.** Writing entry/exit/stop/sizing explicitly, and
  marking what the converter invented, is exactly right.
- **Explicit over-optimisation warning.** *"If you tweak a strategy until it fits the past perfectly,
  it will almost certainly fail in the future."* Stated plainly and correctly.
- **A forward-test ladder**: paper → small live → scale. Identical shape to the gate this project
  built independently, for the same reason.

## What it misses — and these are the interesting parts

1. **Multiplicity is never mentioned.** 325 tests, one winner, no correction, no expected-best-of-N,
   no significance test. This is the whole finding above.
2. **No out-of-sample holdout.** Parameters are chosen on the same data the winner is reported on.
   TradingView re-verification confirms the *arithmetic*, not the *edge* — the same data, re-run
   faithfully, reproduces the same overfit.
3. **The LLM picks the winner from results it produced.** "It will actually tell Claude to highlight
   the strategies it thinks are the best." A model ranking a set it generated is the conflict of
   interest this arena forbids outright — proposing and adjudicating must be separated.
4. **The BONUS is a trap.** *"Add five strategies of your own… use your own reasoning and judgment to
   choose them, without backtesting first."* Those five enter the same ranking on their priors alone.
   It is a fair *experiment* (do a model's trading priors carry signal?) presented as a *feature*.
5. **Nothing is pre-registered.** The strategy set, the metric, the baseline and the winner rule are
   all settled after the results are visible.
6. **n is unreported**, as above.
7. **~6.5 backtests per strategy**, so 325 is not the true number of tries — the effective search is
   wider still.

## The meta-point

53.8K views, 228 reposts, and the article's central evidence is a best-of-325 backtest whose decisive
statistic is withheld. That is not a criticism of the author — who is more careful than the genre,
and who warns readers away from exactly the failure he then falls into — it is a measurement of **how
thin the evaluative infrastructure under this entire field is.** The audience is large, motivated, and
has essentially nothing rigorous to test claims against.

That gap is the arena's actual market.
